# Britain Signs the Reset, and Hands the Bill to Parliament

A week in which the country agreed a new relationship with Europe, was told to abolish leasehold, and watched a five-year-old company become briefly worth two trillion dollars.

## A deal in Brussels, a report in Westminster

The Prime Minister came back from Brussels on Friday with the thing he has wanted since the spring: a signature. It covers food and agricultural exports, linkage of the emissions trading schemes, and a youth experience scheme letting up to 100,000 young Europeans a year live and work here, with the same number of Britons going the other way. The Treasury's figure is up to £17bn a year. Ed Miliband spent Saturday insisting the deal does not depend on the parallel row about tuition fees, the sort of denial that tells you precisely which row is coming. Legislation lands in Parliament this week, when an agreement starts being amended. [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6jreyqgw4e9o)

At home, the government spent the week legislating against things it had spent years describing. Alan Milburn's review, published on Tuesday, is the most consequential domestic document of the autumn: a Youth Opportunity Service with a legal entitlement attached, a named adviser within four months, and the Neet rate halved in a decade, paid for by removing the Universal Credit health top-up and Pip from young adults. Milburn's arithmetic is the argument: £25 on benefits for every £1 on help. On Thursday the leasehold bill arrived, banning most new leasehold flats, capping ground rents at £250 from 2028 and letting them fall to a peppercorn after forty years — an abolition in instalments, with the freeholders' lawyers already booked in.

Underneath it all, the numbers went sideways: inflation at 3.6%, a twelve-month high, dragged up by the pumps, and retail sales up 0.7% only because the weather finally turned and people bought coats. Derby builds trains for a living, so it will notice that from 14 December the line to London runs on hand-me-down rolling stock. Announcements, in other words, landing on an economy that has stopped improving without yet failing.

## Overtime in Antalya

The climate summit was supposed to end on Friday. It did not. COP31 went into a second day of overtime in Antalya on Sunday, with a third draft text published overnight and negotiators called back for what the Turkish presidency called the final session. The substance of the row is simple and has been for thirty years: the draft refers warmly to the Dubai agreement but does not contain the words fossil, oil, coal or phase out anywhere in its ten pages. France called it a step backward. Countries wanting a commitment to move off hydrocarbons are, once again, negotiating with a host who would rather the sentence did not exist. [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cq7827z2841zo) [RFI](https://www.rfi.fr/en/international/20261122-cop31-draft-deal-sparks-backlash-as-france-warns-of-step-backward)

There is a particular irony in watching this from Britain in the week Britain signed an emissions trading link with the European Union. The mechanism by which carbon gets a price is now the part of climate policy that governments find easiest to agree; the part where someone says the words out loud is the part that runs into overtime. A carbon market is a technical arrangement between regulators, and it can be signed in a room in Brussels. A phase-out is a promise to voters and to workers, and it has to be written down where everyone can read it.

The summit's other distinction is less flattering: it is on course to be the longest COP ever held. Delegates in Antalya have now spent more days failing to agree than the Paris conference spent agreeing. Turkey has been running a voluntary pledge on the side that AI should be developed sustainably — a sentence that would have been eccentric at Glasgow and now reads as a description of where the carbon is going. The draft may yet acquire a verb. The decade will not acquire a mechanism.

## Three men in a room in Shenzhen

On Thursday, in Shenzhen, the presidents of the United States, China and Russia sat down together for the first time. Donald Trump had added a one-to-one with Vladimir Putin at short notice, over the objections of his own officials, and the trilateral followed: ninety minutes, a joint declaration on peace, and a press conference in which Putin was asked whether Russia would now end the war in Ukraine and joked that Trump had put the journalist up to it. He did not answer. He said the format had been useful and no overly ambitious goals had been set, which is the most honest summary of a summit anyone gave all week. [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/18/nx-s1-6040432/trump-xi-and-putin-shenzhen-china-russia-usa) [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-20/key-takeaways-from-trump-xi-putin-meeting/107407564)

The intelligence picture published before the meeting was blunter than the communiqué: US agencies assessed that Putin was not ready to negotiate seriously, and would keep fighting. Trump told European leaders he thought the Russian president was tired of the war. Both of those things can be true, and the gap between them is where the war continues.

What has changed is the geography. On Sunday a Russian cargo ship was struck by a drone in the Baltic Sea, and Finland's prime minister said the conflict was "spilling over" — a phrase from a country that joined NATO precisely to stop that sentence applying to it. The declaration in Shenzhen mentions peace. The week's events happened in the water.

## A trillion and a half, and a proof

On Thursday morning a five-year-old company priced its shares at $83, opened at $111.50, briefly touched a $2tn valuation and closed at $97.17 — about $1.77tn, the largest stock market debut in history, on a day when Wall Street fell sharply. By Friday the arguments about Anthropic's business had been replaced by an argument about its mathematics. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by the company, posted a note saying that Claude had helped him prove the Riemann hypothesis, a problem open since 1859. On Saturday afternoon two organisations, the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative, said they had checked the machine-verified proof and had full confidence in it. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/21/anthropic-riemann-hypothesis-solved-claude-ai)

Alpöge apologised for the timing, which was indeed exquisite: the note landed a day after the float, and a company with a share price to defend now has a proof to point at. He was also careful to say that Claude "far outpaced me near the finish line", which is a more interesting sentence than any press release issued this week. Mathematicians are entitled to be uneasy. They have watched two near-trillion-dollar firms spend the autumn announcing results in their field, and the verification here rests on a proof assistant that itself has to be trusted.

The politics is arriving faster than the philosophy. On Friday, the Commons gives a second reading to Alex Sobel's Artificial Superintelligence Bill, which would prohibit the development, deployment and operation of artificial superintelligence systems and establish monitoring powers over them. It is a private member's bill on a Friday, which in Westminster is where ambitious legislation goes to be admired and then dropped. It is also the first time the question of a limit has been put on the order paper at all. [UK Parliament](https://bills.parliament.uk/bills/4288)

## From the week's papers

**A body that is not there.** Time Team's dig in the Garden Field cemetery at Sutton Hoo found a grave where the occupant survives as an impression pressed into the sand — no bone, just the shape of a person, with beads, tweezers, a knife and a brooch, and a second burial beneath. It is the first serious excavation beyond the royal mounds in decades, and it suggests the cemetery was far busier than the ship burial ever implied. [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39w95dnyrlvo)

**Star Trek's books have stopped.** Una McCormack's Deep Space Nine novel *The Peacemakers* came out on 17 November. Pocket Books' catalogue lists nothing after it: no 2027 titles, no contracted freelancers, and an author saying the licence has not been renewed. After forty-seven years of continuous publishing, the franchise's continuity is now being maintained by comics and a children's show that runs three minutes an episode. [Fandom Pulse](https://www.fandompulse.com/p/star-treks-novel-line-has-no-2027)

**The jungle emptied out.** *I'm A Celebrity* returned on Monday to the lowest overnight ratings in its history, having averaged 8.7 million last year. Twenty-odd series in, the format has achieved the thing British television fears most: it is now a fixed point in the calendar that people no longer feel obliged to attend. [Derbyshire Live](https://www.derbytelegraph.co.uk/news/celebs-tv/im-celebrity-plummet-lowest-ever-11250962)

**A pit yard becomes a council office.** Work started this week at Cadley Park, on the former Cadley Colliery site in Swadlincote, on South Derbyshire's new leisure centre and district council headquarters, due at the end of 2028. Nobody will write a column about it. It is nonetheless the sort of regeneration that decides whether a town has a centre in twenty years. [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cm894708j7wyo)

## The week ahead

**Monday 23 November** — Both Houses return after the recess, and the Commons gives time to an e-petition on an independent review of council tax and stamp duty. The Brussels agreement's legislation begins its passage in the same week the government is trying to keep it away from tuition fees.

**Wednesday 25 November** — *Blade Runner 2099* arrives on Prime Video, eight parts, Michelle Yeoh and Hunter Schafer, Ridley Scott executive producing. The Guardian's early verdict was that four hours in, nobody involved seems sure why it exists.

**Friday 27 November** — Second reading of the Artificial Superintelligence Bill, and of the Personal Data (Digital Twins) Bill, on a private members' Friday. Attendance will be thin and the division, if there is one, will be instructive.

**Saturday 28 November** — The Orks' Armoured Blitzmob lands, with allocations already eaten, and Derby switches on its Christmas lights. The two events will between them account for most of the city centre's Saturday afternoon queueing.

The week had a shape, and the shape was paperwork trying to catch up with something that had already happened. A deal in Brussels that must now be legislated; a review that must now be afforded; a leasehold system abolished over forty years because the alternative was compensating somebody; a proof that arrived the day after the listing, from a company that no longer has to persuade anyone of anything. Even the summit in Antalya is a document being edited in public. Most weeks, the news is what happened. This one was mostly about what has been agreed and not yet paid for.
